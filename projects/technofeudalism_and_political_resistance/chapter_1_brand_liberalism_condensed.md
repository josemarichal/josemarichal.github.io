If the right to express oneself is a centerpoint to the experience of liberalism, then that right is not universally enjoyed on social media platforms. Platforms are increasingly using AI to to automate content moderation. Facebook claimed in 2021 that 97 percent of hate speech was detected and flagged by algorithm (cite). This “pre-emptive” regulation of speech (something we’ll discuss in greater detail in chapter 6) is what Armijo (2022) refers to as a shift from ex-post to ex-ante moderation. IN ex-post, the user posts and it is up to the community to report violations of terms of service which is then evaluated by human moderators. This “human in the loop” process of moderation has its disadvantages. Individuals can see horrific images before they are removed. Early Twitter image of Daniel Perl being beheaded by ISIS. This can create significant mental health issues for tech workers.

While ex-post mdoeration had its disadvantgages, it also serves as a form of democratic practice, allowing the community to serve as moderators of their own digital space. In Forrestal’s language it’s a form of “democratic affordance” for the members of a community to take a role in the enforcement of community norms (2022). In reality, social media platforms are too large and unwieldy for users to serve this purpose. Indeed, it cuts against the market imperatives of platforms who don’t want citizens entrusted with civic obligations to police the platforms they use, they want consumers who are free to enjoy the platform unencumbered by democratic obligations. As such, platforms are incentivized to move to ex ante moderation where the user submits content and the AI evaluates and flags or simply removes the content that is deemed against the terms and conditions. This constitutes what Armijo (2021) calls digital prior-restraint. The process of screening before posting before any other users sees the content appears to be a violation of the ocre principle of free speech. We can see the ways in which this can be used to pre-emptively eliminate discourses that an authoritarian government may not want users to see (TikTok rutgers report, current allegations of tiktok fork changing the political content on the algorithm).

Historically there have been that many candidates - religion, art, commerce, Etc. many strive for excellence in one of the social spheres of society. In on Justification, sociologists Luc Boltanski and Laurent Thevenot developed a theory of six different "logics of justification" in public discourse. Each of these had their own logics and standards. They came up with distinct "orders of worth" or "principles of evaluation" that individuals who seek recognition in that area could employ to gain status and legitimacy. They were: civic (equality and solidarity), market (profit), industrial (efficiency), domestic (loyalty), inspiration (intuition and novelty), and fame (attention).

Each of these systems offer competing visions of the good. Hence, each logic values objects differently. For example, a Rolls Royce might carry a different worth in a market or industrial “logics of justification” where it might convey power or status versus a civic or inspiration logic where it might be seen as ostentatious or gaudy.

Using Boltanski and Thevenot's framework, we can see that the social media age has elevated “fame” as a logic, so much so that it has bled into other logics of justification. Politicians, corporations and artist have all at one time or another sought to employ social media to gain relevance.

As other logics of justification as become subsumed by the need to “be seen,” we’ve succumb to the algorithmic ordering of our lives. Out lives aren’t ordered it according to Platonic forms or aristotelian virtue, it does it based on the whims of the “fame” logic. But not just the fame logic, a sense of civic solidarity that had been lost in the neoliberal era. The logic of fame by itself is indifferent to how individuals construct meaning or how that fame is derived. In neoliberalism, the traditional rational actor models suggested that human beings are capable of using reason to coming up with their own preferences and it is the purpose of the market to provide those preferences most efficiently. Those who can meet those needs profit and both parties through pareto optimal outcomes win.

## We have Never Been a Public Sphere

This, of course, cuts against the marketplace of ideas. Schrader (2022) makes a compelling case that the marketplace of ideas was always an Enlightenment-era fiction. He draws on the analogy of Arches National Park, with its thousands of surreal-looking rock formations, as a perfect metaphor for the “marketplace” of ideas. He locates the origin of this metaphor in Oliver Wendell Holmes.

The Enlightenment assumptions of the marketplace of ideas include three core fallacies:

Truth is universal and waiting to be discovered through rational discourse (the Rawlsian overlapping consensus);

People are rational and capable of discerning truth from fiction regardless of the information context;

Human beings are similar enough in their capacities to reason effectively.

These preconditions have always been abstractions.

The public sphere has never been an Algonquin table or a pastoral town square, yet Schrader shows that repeatedly, justices allude to a “town crier on a soapbox” (Reno v. ACLU) or the “public streets and sidewalks” of the Internet (McCullen v. Coakley). Embedded in these metaphors is the belief that the virtual “cafes” and “sidewalks” were universally accessible, structured by norms and rules of rational debate, and premised on the Miltonian hope that “truth vanquishes falsity.” Even if, as Schrader notes, Holmes expressed deep reservations in private correspondence that universal truth was an unknowably “mirage” and ultimately not desired by people who prefer to adhere to Vonnegut’s foma or Wilde’s beautiful untruths.

This unempirical faith led, in Schrader’s view, to support for a maximalist legal policy of marketplace protection where courts repeatedly opted for extending expression from “artificial entities” like corporations on the assumption that truth will deterministically win in the “marketplace.” This unwittingly led to the modern era of algorithmic discourse where synthetic speech by bots or AI tools enjoys protections based on the fabulism of “truth winning out.” The courts in this instance ignore the vast wealth of social science that asserts humans are not “rational” because the racial, gender, ideological, and other filters they use to evaluate the world make absolute certainty about the world impossible.

Rather than think of the world as a marketplace, Schrader proposes a view of speech as “mini-spaces” within a multiverse. This multiplex view of discourse acknowledges the ways in which groups are also discursive communities. Increasingly, these communities are reinforced by engagement and recommendation algorithms and bots that predetermine what they see and amplify discourses that reinforce divisiveness and extremism.

If our assumptions about truth, rationality, and homogeneity are questionable, how do we do liberal democracy? How do we engage in collective decision-making? Agonism? Pragmatism?

## The Turn Towards Groupism

Grouping isn’t experiential, it becomes topological topological: graphs, vectors, statistics. Data doesn't "move" — it is copied, compressed, and identically reproduced.

Epistemic implications (how we know space)

Maps become arguments, not representations. A GIS layer isn't a picture of reality; it's the output of algorithmic operations (overlays, raster algebra, statistical rules). Whoever defines the rules defines what the "space" shows. Two datasets processed differently produce two different geographies.

The map precedes the territory. Since geospace is computed before it's experienced, our perception of place arrives pre-filtered by code. Like the Grand Tourists who needed to re-read classical texts to "see" Rome correctly, we need to understand the algorithm to see the digital city correctly.

Political implications (power and control)

Location becomes an act of surveillance. If position is mathematically computed (GPS timing, signal triangulation), then being located is automatic and unavoidable. The article notes Google, Facebook, and the NSA as hosts of "predictive data fields" — the mathematization of space makes populations legible, predictable, and governable.

Control shifts from territory to infrastructure. Power no longer resides in owning land but in owning the topological layer — protocols, server grids, mapping platforms. Harold Innis's point about media "bias" applies: whoever builds the geometry of process shapes the space everyone else moves in.

Opacity becomes a governance problem. The operative layer (codes, chips, protocols) is invisible behind screens. Most users can't read it, so the forces structuring their spatial experience operate without meaningful public scrutiny — the "black box" problem the article wants media archaeology to open.

Practical/material implications

Distance loses meaning; timing gains it. When data is copied rather than transported, geographic separation matters less than latency. The article's high-frequency trading example is literal: nanoseconds of cable delay have financial value. Space-time collapses into a "spatio-temporal data tissue."

Errors become ontological, not just technical. If geography is computed, then glitches, bad data, or biased training sets don't misrepresent a place — for all practical purposes they are the place. There's no neutral ground to appeal to outside the computation.

Human agency is statistically dissolved. Just as Wiener's stochastic analysis discards the individual particle, geospatial analytics treats the mobile subject as a "nonlocated address." You are a data point in a pattern, not a body in a place.

Conceptual implication

"Geography" becomes a metaphor. The article's sharpest point: once space is a data matrix, the language of geography (location, region, movement) is phenomenological nostalgia — inherited vocabulary describing something that no longer exists underneath. Critical analysis must therefore descend from the map-image to the math.

"Culture is counted rather than narrated." This one line condenses the whole article: cultural meaning once unfolded in duration and story (chronos); now it is produced in discrete, timed operations. The temporal form of culture has changed from narrative to impulse.

Critique must become processual. You can't analyze time-critical media by interpreting their content or their "effects" — you need a dynamic, process-oriented media archaeology that looks at operations as they run (timing diagrams, protocols, latencies), not at artifacts at rest.

A new blind spot for humanism. If the decisive events occur below sensation, then human experience is structurally excluded from the ground level of technological culture. Human perception becomes a slow, downstream surface effect of microseconds it can never witness.

Knowledge itself becomes time-sensitive. Time-criticality is "a new object of epistemological attention in the economy of knowledge" — knowing when (within milliseconds) becomes as important as knowing what.

Ontological enclosure (OE) inhibits the development of phronesis, defined as: “A true state, reasoned, and capable of action with regard to things that are good and bad for man.” In Making Social Science Matter, Bent Flyvbjerg challenges the traditional, context-independent view of positivist science, which misses that facts must be situated in context.
