Course Goals & Overview

The study of American political institutions provides important context for understanding contemporary debates over critical policy issues. This class takes an American Political Development (APD) perspective to structure a semester-long exploration into why our political institutions behave the way they do. The course is organized in three distinct phases:

  1. Current Critiques of American Institutions: An examination of modern dysfunctions, with particular focus on gridlock, partisan sorting, democratic backsliding, and legislative inaction.
  2. The Historical Evolution of the American State: Tracing key developmental ideas and inflection points over time, including the primacy of industry, American exceptionalism, race, and civil rights, with intensive emphasis on constitutional design and federalism.
  3. Theories of Institutional Function & Interaction: Explaining how core American institutions operate—including the legislative branch (House and Senate), executive and bureaucracy, judiciaries, political parties, and interest groups—with special attention paid to inter-branch relationships and coalitions.

Prerequisite Note: Because this is a 400-level Political Science course, a foundational understanding of American government (akin to what one receives in an introductory American government or AP Government course) is assumed.

Course Assignments & Grading

Your semester grade will be determined by performance across four core assignments totaling 100 points:

1. Discussion Leadership • 10 Points

One Friday during the semester, you will be responsible for leading class discussion on the readings assigned for that day. You will lead a discussion that draws out the central theoretical arguments and empirical claims from the readings. To guide your peers, you will prepare a set of thought-provoking questions for group discussion. In addition, you will write and submit a 5–7 page critical analysis paper based on the readings. Questions and responses will be evaluated on intellectual thoughtfulness, rigor, and clarity.

2. Class & Discussion Participation • 10 Points

Prior to class periods, you will post your reflections on the assigned readings as a comment on the class discussion forum. You will be expected to arrive in class prepared to actively discuss your reflections with peers, serving as the springboard for deeper inquiry into the topic. Evaluation focuses on consistent engagement, evidence of completed readings, and critical reflection.

3. Mid-Term & Final Essay Exams • 50 Points (2 × 25 pts)

We will evaluate our study of American political institutions through two comprehensive essay exams requiring you to apply foundational theories and APD concepts to current institutional dilemmas:

  • Exam #1 (Take-Home Essay) • 25 Points: Handed out on March 6; due March 13. Covers foundational definitions of democracy, democratic backsliding, critiques of the state, exceptionalism, and legislative gridlock.
  • Exam #2 (Take-Home Essay) • 25 Points: Handed out on April 26; due May 5. Covers the presidency, bureaucracy, judicial politics, political parties, and interest-group mobilization.

4. Institutions Advocacy Project & Presentation • 30 Points

At the close of the semester, you will author a research-based policy brief addressed to an advocacy organization on a policy issue of your choosing detailing the institutional challenges and opportunities for achieving policy goals at the national level (View Project Guidelines).

Acting as an institutional consultant, you will analyze why past efforts may have stalled (e.g., explaining why gun violence prevention legislation faces persistent congressional gridlock) and provide an actionable roadmap rooted in institutional design, history, and rules. The final paper is 10–15 pages in length, accompanied by a 5–10 minute conference-style presentation.

On Political Power, Hybris, and Democratic Resilience

“The greatest mistake made by those in power, like Darius, was the sin of hybris. That Greek word means 'outrageous arrogance.' Hybris is the outrageous arrogance that marks the abuse of power. Only those invested with enormous power can commit the sin of hybris. Hybris is the imposition of your will, at all costs. The Greeks believed that hybris was preceded by até, or moral blindness that makes you believe that you can do anything you want to and there will be no consequences from either Gods or men.”

A central lesson across American political development: institutions, veto gates, and constitutional checks exist precisely to constrain hybris and protect democratic resilience against moral blindness in governance.
